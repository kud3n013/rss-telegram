In a near unanimous vote, city council members in Cambridge, Massachusetts, adopted a [policy order](https://reason.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/mini-packet_Regular-City-Council-Meeting_20261012152554657.pdf) suggesting that local law enforcement authorities shouldn't arrest or prosecute sex workers.

With eight members voting yes and one voting present during the council's October 5 [meeting](https://cambridgema.iqm2.com/Citizens/Detail_Meeting.aspx?ID=4720), the new policy aims to "let folks in our community know that we do stand in support of not arresting or prosecuting people who sell sex," as Councilor Patricia M. Nolan put it.

The order's stipulation with regard to sex *customers*, however, is unclear. And what practical effect it will have is uncertain, since Cambridge City Council members can't change Massachusetts state law, under which both buying and selling sex are still crimes.

The new policy order states that it is the council's "belief that the arrest of adult persons for consensual sex work should continue to be amongst the lowest law enforcement priorities for the City of Cambridge" and that "the Middlesex County District Attorney should cease prosecution of persons for consensual adult sex work and cease prosecution of persons who provide support services to sex workers absent unlawful means."

The district attorney's office does not have to take this recommendation, of course. Still, it seems meaningful that government officials are urging prosecutors to adopt some level of decriminalization policy when it comes to prostitution. The Cambridge City Council isn't even the first in the area to do so. "In 2019, Somerville City Council [voted to direct](https://www.cambridgeday.com/2024/05/07/bill-to-protect-sex-workers-draws-council-support-in-somerville-following-2019-decriminalization/) Somerville police not to arrest sex workers," *Cambridge Day* [points out](https://www.cambridgeday.com/2026/10/07/policy-order-supporting-sex-workers/).

Originally, some Cambridge authorities wanted to go even further. An [earlier draft](https://reason.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Packet_20261005202346692-Meetings2451Packet_20261005202346692.pdf) of the order urged "the State House to support efforts to achieve the full decriminalization of consensual adult sex work statewide," but that language was removed before passage of the final policy last week. The change was made so as "not [to be] taking a stand on…full decriminalization of the buyer," Nolan said at the meeting.

Yet [according](https://csindie.com/cambridge-decriminalizes-sex-work-within-its-borders-but-clients-arent-included-in-the-exemption/) to *The Cambridge Somerville Independent*, Council Member Jivan Sobrinho-Wheeler, who authored the new policy, said that even as adopted it still supports full decriminalization (that is, decriminalization of both full-service sex work providers and their clients):

> In the version that passed, some councillors believed they voted for something different: that customers remain seen as criminals and that the goal is to end sex work rather than allow the trade to continue safely with protections for human and worker rights. "People are going to read it a little differently," Sobrinho-Wheeler said, but he and supporters believe the order continues to cover clients and suppliers of sex work.

Sex worker rights advocates are calling the policy—which also includes plans for a phone line that sex workers can use to report crimes against them without having to interact with police—a win.

"Victories seem rare. This one is special because of how many sex workers, survivors, and allies supported our effort," Mary Carol, co-chair of the Boston Sex Workers and Allies Collective, said in a [press release](https://reason.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/PRESS-RELEASE-Cambridge-Policy-Order1.docx).

During the public comment portion of [the council's October 5 meeting](https://cambridgema.v3.swagit.com/videos/402956), at least 27 people—including current and former sex workers, lawyers, and people in social services—spoke in support of full decriminalization.

"I choose to do this work and I choose to do this work without going to jail for it," said Rita Romero, who said she has been "a sex worker for over 20 years."

A woman describing herself as "a student, a mother, an advocate, and a sex worker" told council members: "We are not asking you to understand every choice that we make. We are asking you to understand that criminalization does not make us disappear, it only changes how safely we are able to exist."

"Our commonwealth should protect people's dignity, not punish them for trying to survive," opined [Kayla Kittredge](https://ballotpedia.org/Kayla_Kittredge), an independent candidate for lieutenant governor of Massachusetts.

Several speakers who described themselves as survivors of sex trafficking supported the policy order. This included Quinn, who said she was "pushed into the sex industry as a teenager" and still supports full decriminalization.

Clients "didn't hurt me because of a lack of punishment," Quinn said. "They were able to hurt me because I had a lack of power, political power, to keep myself safe and to determine the trajectory of my own life. The fact is full decriminalization—not partial, not the equality model—only full decriminalization has been proven to afford both trafficking victims and sex workers more political power, actual political power."

Only three people—two with the group Safe Exit, which helps people who do not want to be selling sexual services to stop doing so—spoke against the policy.

Safe Exit's Audra Dooty said the policy would "hurt people and get more vulnerable folks sucked into the life" and advocated what's alternately known as the Nordic model, the equality model, or partial decriminalization, in which sex workers are not criminalized but their clients are.

Several speakers at the council meeting directly opposed any policy that still prioritizes arresting sex buyers. Claudia Torres, a staff attorney at Justice at Work, noted that while some say decriminalization removes protection for vulnerable people, "vulnerability does not make meaningful consent impossible. Whether coercion is present is a fact-intensive, context-specific inquiry."

"Criminalizing buyers does not eliminate dangerous clients or exploitation," said Molly Lynch, a licensed clinical social worker. Instead, it "makes it harder for sex workers to protect themselves from those dangers." Lynch added that having worked with street-based sex workers, she learned that "sex workers are experts in their own safety."

"In other industries, like domestic work, we say the solution is giving people more labor protections," Jessica Van Meer told the council. "We don't say the solution to trafficking of domestic workers is to criminalize hiring a maid."

The new Cambridge policy order puts on the record what already seems to be police policy. Shameka Gregory with the city's Domestic and Gender-Based Violence Prevention Initiative said the Cambridge Police Department has made only about four sex worker arrests in the past five years. She did not say if any or how many solicitation arrests were made.

But even if local police aren't proactively going after prostitution, sex workers and/or their customers in the area may still face arrests and prosecutions. In recent years, Cambridge has been the home of a [high-profile "brothel" bust](https://www.boston.com/news/the-boston-globe/2026/10/05/they-were-charged-with-paying-for-sex-in-the-cambridge-brothel-ring-and-paid-a-unique-price/) involving both federal and state police. More than 30 clients were arrested and prosecuted on state charges, and three people who ran the business were prosecuted on federal charges.
