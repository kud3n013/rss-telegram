From [*State v. Elavsky*](https://mncourts.gov/_media/migration/appellate/court-of-appeals/standard-opinions/12oct26/OPa251929-101226.pdf), decided yesterday by Minnesota Court of Appeals Judge Kevin Ross, joined by Judge Elise Larson:

> Neil Elavsky expressed his opposition to a social cause that was the subject of an outdoor fundraising event by posting leaflets on the windshields of cars near the event, and he confronted a supporter of the cause after he saw her removing his posted leaflets. He rushed toward her, demanded she return the removed leaflets to him, and grabbed them from her hand when she refused.
>
> After the woman claimed that Elavsky scratched her finger when he snatched the leaflets from her, the state charged him with fifth-degree assault (fear), fifth-degree assault (harm), and disorderly conduct. A jury acquitted him of assault (harm) but found him guilty on the other charges, and the district court convicted him of assault (fear). Because the record contains insufficient circumstantial evidence to prove that Elavsky intended to cause the woman fear of immediate bodily harm, we reverse the assault conviction ….
>
> Up to 5,000 supporters, including the employees of business sponsors, of the National Alliance of Mental Illness (NAMI) gathered at Minnehaha Park in Minneapolis one morning in September 2023 to participate in a fundraising march. An altercation at the event resulted in the state charging Neil Elavsky with fifth-degree assault (fear), fifth-degree assault (harm), and disorderly conduct. At Elavsky's trial, the jury heard evidence of the following account.
>
> Two NAMI supporters, whom we will call "June" and "Lynn," names we have randomly chosen in the interest of their privacy, were employees of a large health-insurance company and arrived early to set up a picnic area and booth to greet their participating coworkers and provide information about their employer. Elavsky, who opposes NAMI's cause, was also present at the event. Elavsky and a companion had previously protested NAMI by distributing windshield leaflets that criticized NAMI and urged readers not to donate to it.
>
> Lynn, who "[does not] agree with what the flyers were stating" and had remembered Elavsky and his companion from their activity at prior NAMI events, had previously complained about the leaflets to NAMI's executive director. She saw Elavsky's companion at about noon distributing leaflets and asked him not to place a leaflet on her car. June saw Elavsky during the event, "shouting very, very loudly" with a "big, full, bold voice."
>
> The event neared its end at about 2:00 p.m., and Lynn saw that her car had been tagged with a leaflet. She asked June to remove the leaflet from her car and from a different person's car. June did so, and she started walking toward a trash can. Elavsky approached June from behind saying, "Give me those" and "That's private property. Give me that back." He appeared to June to be "very agitated" and, according to her, spoke to her in the same "big, bold, boisterous" voice she had heard him using earlier.
>
> June turned toward Elavsky, but she did not hand him the leaflets. Instead, she kept them in her hand at her side. She argued, "It's not private property. It's been distributed. It's public property." Elavsky "lurched" toward June, who recounted, "[A]ll of a sudden I felt his arms going around me, reaching for the flyers that he claimed were private property." At that point, the two were chest to chest, and June could feel Elavsky's breath on her face. She said, "[Elavsky] grabbed my hand and my arm … to grab those flyers out of my hand." She said that he "grabbed the two flyers that [she] had in [her] hand, out of [her] hand, and then he turned to walk away." She acknowledged that Elavsky never shoved, hit, or raised his arms toward her.
>
> The prosecutor asked June at least five times how or what she felt during her encounter with Elavsky. She answered that she "really didn't have any feelings"; "[k]ind of more of a shock than anything else"; "not understanding why this gentleman is approaching me like this"; "angry"; "very violated." Even after the prosecutor clearly tried leading June to the answer he was fishing for, with, "[June], as far as being fearful, did you feel fear at that moment," June still did not say that she feared Elavsky would harm her during the incident….
>
> The court held that there was insufficient evidence that Elavsky acted "with intent to cause fear in another of immediate bodily harm," which was required for the assault-by-fear charge (Elavsky hadn't contested his conviction on a disorderly conduct charge):
>
> The state did not offer direct evidence that Elavsky had this specific intent, relying instead on circumstantial evidence. When an element of a conviction rests on circumstantial evidence, we … [consider] whether the reasonable inferences drawn from the circumstances proved are, as a whole, not only consistent with the defendant's guilt but also "inconsistent with any rational hypothesis other than guilt." Without any deference to the verdict, if we conclude that the circumstances proved allow for a reasonable hypothesis inconsistent with guilt, the evidence is insufficient and we will reverse the conviction….
>
> The state is correct that the circumstances proved reasonably support an inference that Elavsky intended to cause June fear of immediate bodily harm. We can infer that a person intends the natural and probable consequences of his actions. Elavsky's angry demeanor, his leafletting and loud expressions of disagreement with NAMI's cause throughout the day, his lurching approach toward June while demanding that she return the leaflets to him, and his abrupt entry into her personal space to the point of chest-to-chest contact would together allow a reasonable person to infer that he might have specifically intended his actions to cause June to be immediately afraid that he would injure her.
>
> That June did not testify that Elavsky's actions in fact caused her to fear immediate injury—despite multiple failed attempts by the prosecutor—is not a basis for us to conclude otherwise. This is because evidence that the victim felt fear is neither necessary nor sufficient to prove that the defendant intended to cause fear….
>
> But we are likewise satisfied that the circumstances proved also support a reasonable inference other than guilt. Elavsky argues convincingly that one can reasonably infer from the same circumstances that he intended only to retrieve the leaflets from June and nothing more. Elavsky is a committed NAMI protester who distributed hundreds of leaflets on the day of the incident, just as he had in the past.
>
> The uncontradicted testimony established that he had already been loud and angry in his protesting long before he saw June remove the leaflets. He demanded that June return what he described as his property and grabbed them from her after she disagreed about who owned them. June described his actions in reaching around her only as intended "to try to get the flyer[s]" from her.
>
> Elavsky ended his encounter with June immediately after he got the leaflets back, turning promptly to walk away. June then moved in front of Elavsky, directly confronting him about her finger. All the circumstances considered as a whole support the alternative inference that Elavsky's only actual intent was to get the leaflets back from June. This is a reasonable inference of a hypothesis other than guilt.

Judge Lisa Beane dissented:

> Elavsky was agitated and shouting when he approached June—a woman he did not know—and demanded that she give him two flyers she was holding in her hand that Elavsky claimed were rightfully his. When she did not immediately do so, Elavsky wrapped both his arms around her body so that their chests were touching, and she could feel his breath on her face. He grabbed her arm, and he forcibly took the flyers from her hand. Although June did not use the word "fear" to describe how Elavsky's conduct made her feel, she described feeling the need to defend herself against his aggressive behavior and feeling unnerved and disconcerted. Elavsky is physically larger than June in both height and weight.
>
> These circumstances, I believe, foreclose the possibility that Elavsky did not intend to cause June fear—that is, they are inconsistent with any reasonable hypothesis other than guilt. Elavsky argues that the circumstances support "the reasonable inference that he exclusively intended to retrieve his flyers without causing fear." Elavsky clearly intended to retrieve the flyers, but the only rational inference from the means he chose to achieve that goal is that he intended to cause June fear of bodily harm.
>
> As the majority notes, we can infer that a person intends the natural and probable consequences of their actions. The natural and probable consequence of an agitated, larger man approaching a smaller woman who is a stranger to him, addressing her in a "very loud" and "intimidating" voice, demanding that she do as he says, and then forcefully grabbing around her body with both arms is that the woman will experience fear of immediate bodily harm. Given Elavsky's actions, it is not reasonable to infer that he intended a different result.
>
> Stated differently, the only reasonable inference regarding Elavsky's intent that can be drawn from the evidence presented at trial, when viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, is the one the jury drew: that Elavsky acted with the purpose of causing, or with the belief that he would cause, June fear of bodily harm.

The majority responded:

> The dissent acknowledges that the circumstances proved demonstrate that "Elavsky clearly intended to retrieve the flyers" but posits that causing June to fear bodily harm was "the means he chose to achieve that goal." We are confident instead that the circumstances proved as we have identified them, or as the dissent has listed them, do not eliminate the reasonable inference that retrieving the flyers was his exclusive objective. The dissent points out that "Elavsky was agitated and shouting when he approached" June. But the evidence showed that he was agitated and shouting both long before the confrontation began and after it ended, strongly suggesting that his agitation and shouting had a purpose wholly independent of causing fear.
>
> The dissent highlights Elavsky's size relative to June's, but his size by itself says nothing about intent to cause fear and nothing in the record indicates that he intended to use his size in any particular way. The dissent especially emphasizes that Elavsky got physically close to June and forcefully and immediately removed the flyers from her—circumstances that most clearly show that he intended to retrieve the flyers from June by force, not that he intended to retrieve them by fear. Again, although one might reasonably infer that Elavsky wanted to cause June to fear bodily harm, we simply disagree with the dissent's conclusion that this is "the only rational inference." …

Erik I. Withall (St. Paul Assistant Public Defender) represents defendant.
