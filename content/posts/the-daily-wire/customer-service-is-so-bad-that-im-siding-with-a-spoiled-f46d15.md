All these years later, you have to give credit to “American Idol.” It was billed as a talent show that would produce a pop star everyone would talk about for decades — a program that would create the next Elvis, with a legion of fans who obsess over the star’s every move.

While the show never produced any talent on par with Elvis — and produced very few contestants even on par with the typical Elvis impersonator you’d find on the side of the road in Vegas — it did manage to create Kelly Clarkson. Before the monoculture died, some 20 million people tuned in to watch Clarkson win the title of “American Idol” in September 2002.

Kelly Clarkson has somehow endured despite the fact that she always seemed like an AI-generated musician from an age before AI — with song lyrics that are literally clichés like “What doesn’t kill you makes you stronger.” Probably in part because she is actually a talented singer, which is a rarity in pop music these days. So she still has a large, dedicated fan base in the year 2026 — the kind of following based on a shared monoculture event that’s very difficult to replicate today, now that everyone has their own personalized algorithm.

In fact, Clarkson’s influence is apparently so significant that she can command the attention of the entire world with a very run-of-the-mill social media post about a bad customer service experience she recently endured. Here’s what she [wrote](https://x.com/kellyclarkson/status/2108998378675933224?s=20) on Saturday:

> *Just drove all the way to Brooklyn to check out this store I thought looked really rad and had a few things in mind to buy and literally was looked at as if I couldn’t afford it and was told he could find something comparable for me. Wow, dude. You’re an idiot and just lost a huge sale. But y’all should go check out Art of Play in Brooklyn if you enjoy being treated like Julia Roberts character in Pretty Woman. For sure, check it out. [Thumbs down emoji.]*

When I saw the post, along with 6 billion other people, my first reaction was to take Kelly Clarkson’s side. That’s not because I care about Kelly Clarkson, know anything about her, know anything about this incident, or care about this incident.

[![](https://dw-wp-production.imgix.net/2025/11/Walsh-Banner.avif)](https://www.dailywire.com/episode/matt-walsh-show-ep-1857)

I choose to believe that, in truth, the reason this post resonated with so many people has nothing to do with Clarkson or her music and everything to do with the fact that pretty much everyone else has come to the same realization.

Whether you’re at an airport, ordering food, or dealing with health insurance — whatever it is, the experience is noticeably worse than it was in the pre-COVID era. So when Kelly Clarkson decided to have a meltdown over this bougie toy store in Brooklyn, many of us were predisposed to agree with her. Not because she’s a celebrity, but because she’s a customer. We know that customers are generally treated like garbage everywhere, in almost every store or restaurant in America, and it’s only getting worse.

The collapse of customer service in this country needs to be studied, so we’re going to take a close look at what’s happening, at a systemic level, to make customer service so utterly terrible.
