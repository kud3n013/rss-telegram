In fourteen hundred ninety-two, **Christopher Columbus** sailed the ocean blue … just to catch strays 534 years later from an affluent white liberal woman at a sold-out country show in Dallas.

During her Sunday night performance at American Airlines Center, country music superstar **Kacey Musgraves** took a moment to [lambast the Italian explorer](https://www.foxnews.com/media/musician-kacey-musgraves-condemns-columbus-the-lamest-person-probably-ever-exist), calling him “the lamest person to probably ever exist” while celebrating Indigenous Peoples’ Day.

The gleeful crowd at Musgraves’ show cheered, and once the video hit social media, yet another musician found herself in the middle of America’s endless culture war.

Which raises an interesting question: **Should musicians keep their political opinions to themselves?**

At a recent show at the Las Vegas Sphere, **Metallica** frontman **James Hetfield** thanked the crowd for joining them and said the heavy metal band has no political or social agenda to share.

“Metallica is not here to influence you with their bullshit,” Hetfield said. “Metallica’s agenda is love, energy, and heavy music.”

While I certainly appreciate Hetfield’s apolitical preference on stage, and I understand the argument for keeping art and politics separate, I don’t entirely agree.

Politics and art go hand-in-hand.

The best artists are passionate, thoughtful, emotionally-driven people with deeply held convictions that inform their work.

From **Bob Dylan**‘s protest anthems to **Toby Keith**’s fiercely patriotic country Americana, music has always served as a vehicle for political and cultural expression.

Imagine how boring music would be if artists had to avoid writing what they feel. If there’s one thing I know for sure, it’s that we don’t need any more **Morgan Wallens**.

### **Here’s the difference**

While I do believe politics and art can exist together, there’s a major difference between expressing your beliefs through the work and delivering a corny, giggly sermon to a captive audience.

Musgraves has never exactly hidden her politics. Her breakout 2013 hit “Follow Your Arrow” explored themes of LGBTQ+ acceptance and sexual exploration — all while most of her traditional country audience hummed along in ignorance.

That’s the beauty of music. It’s subjective and objective all at once. One listener hears Musgraves’ message about sexual autonomy while another taps his boot to the catchy melody.

Hell, it took some people decades to realize that a band literally named **Rage Against the Machine** was protesting structural corporate and government greed.

*Side note:* I think I listened to “Evil Empire” 10,000 times while shooting hoops in the backyard in the summer of 1996. What an album. Still, 7-year-old me didn’t really understand what **Tom Morello** and the boys were on about until the “Bulls on Parade” video dropped

When artists step away from their instruments and start explicitly lecturing audiences about politics, there’s no more room for interpretation.

**And that choice comes with consequences**

Ask the **Dixie Chicks**, whose criticism of President **George W. Bush** in 2003 sparked a massive country music backlash. Or **Kid Rock**, whose outspoken conservative politics have become inseparable from his public persona.

Kid Rock has largely embraced the audience his politics attract. The Chicks dropped the “Dixie,” deepening their connection with the liberal left.

Musgraves is entitled to do the same, but she shouldn’t expect every longtime fan to follow her.

In 2026, everyone has a mouthpiece and an apparent calling to use it. But your audience isn’t obligated to subsidize your worldview. Choosing not to support Musgraves now that her politics are squarely in your face is a perfectly reasonable reaction.

Artists have every right to make music their way and speak their minds when compelled. Fans can decide if the art is worth the baggage.

So far, the Musgraves fallout is merely on social media. It remains to be seen whether she’ll keep speaking out, and how that will impact record sales or tour attendance.

Whether the impending answer to that equation is worth it will probably tell us everything we need to know about her and her convictions in the years to come.
