The sexual-assault allegations against seven fraternity members at Cornell University have revived a [decades-old](https://www.city-journal.org/article/the-campus-rape-myth) debate about whether college campuses face a rape crisis. A former Cornell student, identified by the pseudonym Jane Doe, says the men sexually assaulted her during a night of heavy drinking and drug use in 2024.

Though the allegations remain [contested](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/01/nyregion/cornell-rape-accusation-reporting.html), journalists, activists, and Democratic politicians have already treated them as fact, citing the case as evidence of a larger problem at American colleges and universities. Vox [says](https://www.vox.com/today-explained-newsletter/504570/cornell-university-sexual-assault-case) that there is “[a] crisis that goes far beyond Cornell.” The Atlantic [asks](https://www.theatlantic.com/culture/2026/10/cornell-gang-rape-allegations/688849) whether college is becoming less safe for women. The Guardian [declares](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/oct/03/cornell-rape-case-male-supremacy) that we live in a “rape-prone society.” Tens of thousands of women on TikTok have [posted](https://www.tiktok.com/tag/iamjanedoe) #IAmJaneDoe in solidarity with the Cornell accuser, drawing [comparisons](https://katiecouric.com/news/national-news/iamjanedoe-social-media-movement-cornell-7) to the #MeToo movement. New York Governor Kathy Hochul is using the case to push for changing state law, [arguing](https://x.com/GovKathyHochul/status/2106047944507998704?s=20) for closing the “voluntary intoxication loophole,” and has appointed Attorney General Letitia James to reinvestigate the allegations.

Conventional crime measures do not support the claim that America is experiencing a rape epidemic. The Bureau of Justice Statistics’ National Crime Victimization Survey, which includes crimes never reported to police, [estimates](https://ncvs.bjs.ojp.gov/year-to-year-comparison/crimeType) that the rape and sexual-assault rate fell from 4.3 victimizations per 1,000 people age 12 or older in 1993 to 2.0 in 2024.

What *has* increased in recent years is the range of experiences now described as nonconsensual. Spearheaded by activists, these expanded definitions — and the subsequent encouragement of women to reinterpret past experiences through them — has created serious consequences for individuals, society, and now potentially also the legal meanings of rape and sexual assault.

Psychologist Nick Haslam [coined](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/295247201_Concept_Creep_Psychology's_Expanding_Concepts_of_Harm_and_Pathology) the term “concept creep” to describe how harm-related terms have expanded “horizontally” to encompass qualitatively different experiences and “vertically” to include less severe forms. Applying this framework to concepts like trauma, bullying, racism, and abuse, he found that heightened sensitivity to perceived harm was most commonly [associated](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886919302387) with a liberal worldview.

The definition of sexual nonconsent has similarly expanded. Once [understood](https://ucr.fbi.gov/crime-in-the-u.s/2011/crime-in-the-u.s.-2011/violent-crime/forcible-rape) in terms of force and incapacity, it began to [change](https://le.fbi.gov/cjis-division/cjis-link/ucr-program-changes-definition-of-rape) with the rise of “affirmative consent.” Still a fringe and widely mocked idea when it [appeared](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/07311214221100836) on college campuses in the 1990s, affirmative consent replaced the familiar “no means no” [standard](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2009-nov-27-la-et-amy27-2009nov27-story.html) with a [requirement](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/means-enough-college-campuses) for explicit, ongoing agreement to sexual activity.

Many regard this change as positive, but it has opened the door to broader interpretations of consent — and nonconsent. Today, nonconsent can cover [silence](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/relationships/sexual-consent), insufficient [enthusiasm](https://www.canada.ca/en/women-gender-equality/campaigns/gender-based-violence-its-not-just/gender-based-violence-resources/learning-modules-youth/module5.html), any level of [intoxication](https://survivors.org/what-is-consent), or feeling [pressured](https://rainn.org/share-the-facts/consent-101-respect-boundaries-and-building-trust), [deceived](https://www.bwss.org/31-actions-for-gender-justice-action-4-normalize-a-culture-of-consent), or [influenced](https://www.loveisrespect.org/resources/what-is-sexual-coercion) to engage in sexual activity.

It took another two decades for affirmative consent to move into the mainstream. By the early 2010s, campus sexual-assault activists had gained a powerful federal foothold through the Obama administration’s [expansion](https://www.ed.gov/media/document/dear-colleague-letter-sexual-violence-background-summary-and-fast-facts-rescinded-april-4-2011-21440.pdf) of Title IX enforcement. In 2014, the administration [created](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/realitycheck/the-press-office/2014/01/22/memorandum-establishing-white-house-task-force-protect-students-sexual-a) the White House Task Force to Protect Students from Sexual Assault and made campus sexual assault a national priority.

Policymakers largely framed the emerging affirmative-consent guidance as prevention: establishing clearer rules for future sexual encounters. But some activists saw another use for it. A broader definition of nonconsent could identify more women as victims, including those who had not previously understood their experiences as rape or sexual assault.

Laura Dunn, founder of SurvJustice, a national nonprofit that provides legal services and advocacy for victims of sexual violence, and Harvard graduate student Rory Gerberg made that argument in [comments](https://www.docsity.com/en/docs/defining-consent-to-effectively-address-sexual/8990389) to the Task Force. They wrote that “survivors frequently fail to identify rape and sexual assault” because society misunderstands consent. Their proposed remedy was “[i]nstitutionalizing an affirmative definition of consent,” with the explicit goal of producing “a legal shift in our societal understanding of consent.”

The nationwide pivot to the affirmative consent [standard](https://www.thehotline.org/resources/what-is-healthy-consent) happened around 2015. That year, Planned Parenthood [called](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/about-us/newsroom/press-releases/planned-parenthood-federation-of-america-calls-for-a-culture-of-consent-highlights-need-for-better-prevention-and-response-to-se) for a national “culture of consent” and, the following year, introduced its FRIES model, which says consent must be “freely given, reversible, informed, enthusiastic, and specific.” Earlier Planned Parenthood guidance [warned](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/blog/can-alcohol-affect-a-guys-ability-to-get-an-erection-what-about-other-drugs) that alcohol lowered inhibitions and made people “less picky” about sexual partners, potentially leading to regret. Its current [guidance](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/relationships/sexual-consent) says a person cannot give consent if he or she is drunk or high.

Similarly, the Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network [says](https://rainn.org/share-the-facts/consent-101-respect-boundaries-and-building-trust) consent must be given “clearly, freely, and enthusiastically,” and that capacity includes being “sober and alert.” Many organizations, [including](https://www.cdc.gov/nisvs/faq/index.html) the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, identify [additional](https://rainn.org/strategies-to-reduce-risk-increase-safety/how-to-respond-if-someone-is-pressuring-you) categories of “sexual violence,” such as “sexual coercion,” which can [include](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/blog/sexual-boundaries-how-to-spot-sexual-coercion) persistent requests for sex or being “worn down” into consenting.

The momentum behind these changes came from claims that colleges were experiencing an epidemic of sexual assault hidden from ordinary crime statistics. The Task Force’s April 2014 [report](https://www.winthrop.edu/uploadedFiles/titleIX/NotAlone-WhiteHouseReportOnStudentSexualAssault.pdf) opened by declaring that “[o]ne in five women is sexually assaulted in college” but later acknowledged that many women whom researchers classified as victims of acquaintance rape “don’t call what happened to them rape.” The Obama administration treated that gap as evidence that assaults were going unrecognized. Ordinary reports could not reveal “what’s really happening on campus,” it argued, so colleges should administer behavioral surveys capable of identifying assaults that women themselves had not regarded as such.

The “one in five” statistic went on to become one of the most [commonly](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/about-us/newsroom/press-releases/planned-parenthood-federation-of-america-calls-for-a-culture-of-consent-highlights-need-for-better-prevention-and-response-to-se) [cited](https://rainn.org/red-zone/campus-sexual-violence-statistics) [estimates](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2014/sep/24/rape-sexual-assault-ban-frats) of campus sexual assault and continues to be [repeated](https://womenshealth.gov/relationships-and-safety/sexual-assault-and-rape/college-sexual-assault) today. Yet it did not come from a national study of campus rape. It originated in a [2007 survey](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/grants/221153.pdf) of students at just two universities, and only 42% of the women asked to participate responded.

By 2014, two of the study’s researchers, Christopher Krebs and Christine Lindquist, were concerned enough about the statistic’s use that they wrote a TIME [article](https://time.com/3633903/campus-rape-1-in-5-sexual-assault-setting-record-straight) to set the record straight. They acknowledged that the figure was “not a nationally representative estimate,” cautioned against using it as a national baseline for campus sexual assault, and warned that the low response rate could have biased their results. They also emphasized that “one in five” did not mean that one in five women had been raped, since their figure included unwanted kissing and touching.

Other research in this area suffers from many of the same methodological flaws that Heather Mac Donald [raised](https://www.city-journal.org/article/the-campus-rape-myth) concerns about in City Journal nearly two decades ago. These can include broad definitions that conflate rape with less serious forms of unwanted physical contact, subjective self-reports, and low response rates that can produce biased samples. Cornell University’s 2025 [survey](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/blogs.cornell.edu/dist/5/7756/files/2025/10/2025-SARM-Survey-report_508.pdf), for example, reported that 35 percent of undergraduate women had experienced “sexual assault.” Its response rate was just [13%](https://president.cornell.edu/initiatives/task-force-sa/tfsa-final-report-2026.pdf), and it relied on a broad measure that grouped very different experiences under the same label.

There’s nothing wrong with telling young people to communicate more clearly and avoid sexual activity with severely impaired partners, but there *can* be issues with encouraging them to apply a new standard of consent to past experiences. When the #MeToo hashtag exploded in October 2017, [millions](https://metoomvmt.org/stay-informed/media-kit) of women began publicly doing just that. These [online](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15248380211043831) [disclosures](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0886260520905095) typically receive sympathy and validation, can attract hundreds of thousands of views, and offer entry into a “survivor” community built around a positive social identity.

Retrospective reclassification of sexual encounters became a recognizable genre of writing. In a 2018 essay, for example, Laura Amy [reinterpreted](https://www.critic.co.nz/features/article/7332/that-one-time-i-realised-it-wasnt-just-a-bad-sex-e) a sexual encounter to which she had explicitly agreed. “I did say yes,” she wrote, and for years she had regarded such experiences as simply bad sex. But after reading #MeToo accounts from women who had felt pressured or obligated to agree, she reconsidered what had happened and concluded, “that is not consensual sex.” A woman [writing](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2021/aug/26/unacknowledged-the-sexual-assault-survivors-who-hide-their-trauma-even-from-themselves) in the Guardian in 2021 likewise reconsidered an encounter she had long regarded as “bad sex” or a “grey-area” experience. After #MeToo, she saw it differently: “That was sexual assault.”

Researchers have documented the same phenomenon. A 2021 [study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8713172) followed 2,566 college students before and after #MeToo. Of the 596 whose experiences fit the researchers’ broad definition of sexual assault, participants were more likely over time to label encounters as sexual assault that they hadn’t originally described in those terms. The authors linked the participants’ changed perceptions to #MeToo. Similarly, a 2023 [study](https://linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/S8755461523000014) found that five of 10 participants said #MeToo led them to reclassify earlier experiences as sexual abuse or assault. The researchers described the process as “reinterpreting, or relabeling experiences as sexual assault.”

In a 2025 [study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12176398) of 380 college students whose experiences met the researchers’ broad criteria for sexual assault, 36% were more likely to call the encounter sexual assault than they remembered it being shortly after it happened. Among that group, 43% said other people had played a role in changing how they understood it.

Women aren’t necessarily fabricating false allegations consciously. A woman may sincerely come to view past encounters in a different light after adopting a new framework for understanding them. Autobiographical memory is [reconstructive](https://wires.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/wcs.1620) rather than a literal recording of the past and is highly open to influence and suggestion, which can lead to [false memories](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5248593). In two experiments [published](https://www.joibs.org/index.php/joibs/article/view/64) in 2026, psychologists Lawrence Patihis and Harry Carter randomly prompted some participants to think more negatively about a former first-date partner, then asked them to recall how they had felt during the date. Those given the negative prompt recalled feeling less safe and more coerced than other participants.

Whether intentional or not, false accusations carry serious consequences. Exposure to false or dubious accusations may make people less willing to believe genuine ones. In one [experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9136472), men who read a news report about a false rape allegation were more skeptical of a separate sexual-abuse complainant and viewed her experience as less harmful.

Supporters of the “believe women” approach often say that false accusations are rare, but a recent Aporia [analysis](https://www.aporiamagazine.com/p/how-common-are-false-rape-accusations) contends that the low rates commonly [cited](https://nsvrc.org/resource/false-reports-moving-beyond-issue-successfully-investigate-and-prosecute-non) are misleading. Such estimates count only accusations proven false while including unresolved, withdrawn, and “unfounded/baseless” cases in the denominator — which can undercount false accusations and make the rate appear artificially low. Moreover, studies of false reports typically rely on police records, making them a poor basis for assessing #MeToo-style accusations that never reach the police but can still carry serious social and reputational consequences for the accused.

Broadening the definitions of sexual assault affects not just individuals but society, too. That includes making ordinary dating and sexual interactions tougher to navigate. In 2020, Pew [found](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2020/08/20/public-attitudes-about-todays-dating-landscape) that 65% of Americans (69% of men and 61% of women) believed the heightened focus on sexual harassment and assault had made it harder for men to know how to interact with someone on a date. Nearly half of Americans said dating itself was more difficult than it was a decade earlier.

A sexual ethic based on a clear “no” asks men to respect women’s choices. The expanding definition of nonconsent imposes a newer standard, asking men to judge whether a woman is sober enough, enthusiastic enough, sufficiently free from pressure, and unlikely to feel differently about the encounter later. It can also encourage women to reinterpret regretted sexual experiences as evidence that someone wronged them. We should be cautious about teaching women to second-guess their choices after the fact—and even more cautious about writing that uncertainty into law.
