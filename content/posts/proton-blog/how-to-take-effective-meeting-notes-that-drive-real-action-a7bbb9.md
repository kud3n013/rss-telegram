It’s an all-too-common scenario: Your team agrees on something in your weekly meeting, but a week later you realize nobody wrote it down and nobody followed up on it.

Focused, actionable meeting notes give your team a shared record of decisions and responsibilities, including enough context for people who missed the discussion.

In this guide, we’ll share how to:

- Choose between meeting notes and formal meeting minutes
- Prepare for a meeting and capture the main discussion points, decisions, and actions
- Organize, share, and follow up on your notes
- Assess AI meeting assistants for privacy and check their summaries for accuracy

## What are meeting notes, and how do they differ from minutes?

Meeting notes summarize what was discussed, what was decided, and who agreed to do what next.

For meetings that need an official record, such as board or committee meetings, use meeting minutes. Minutes follow a formal structure and record proceedings such as motions, votes, and resolutions, along with decisions and assigned actions.

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | **Meeting notes** | **Meeting minutes** |
| **Format** | Informal, flexible | Formal, following a set template |
| **Intended audience** | Internal, team members and collaborators | Stakeholders, board, compliance |
| **Function** | To provide an action-oriented reference for internal teams | An official record of proceedings that can be used for auditing, legal purposes |
| **Typical content** | Records attendees, agenda, key points of discussion, agreements, and action items | Attendance, motions, votes, resolutions, and assigned actions |
| **Best for** | 1:1s, project kickoffs, brainstorming sessions | Board meetings, formal committee meetings, public hearings |

## What meeting notes should include

Include a descriptive title and the meeting date, and use a consistent layout for similar meetings. Organize the main content around the four A’s:

- **Agenda:** List the topics the meeting was intended to cover.
- **Attendance:** Record who attended and, where relevant, who was absent.
- **Agreements:** Explain what was decided and the main reasons behind it.
- **Action items:** Record the agreed tasks, with a named owner and deadline for each one.

## How to take meeting notes

The following steps help you prepare, capture the main points, and turn them into a document your team can use.

### 1. Prepare your agenda

Ask the organizer to share the agenda before the meeting so participants have time to prepare questions, research topics, and suggest anything else that needs discussing.

Add a descriptive title, the date, and each agenda item to your note-taking document as a heading. Leave space underneath for discussion points, decisions, and actions so you’re ready to take notes when the conversation starts.

If there’s no agenda, ask the organizer what the meeting needs to achieve. A discussion about possible approaches needs different notes from a meeting where the team must choose one.

### 2. Record attendance

Record who attends the meeting and, where relevant, their role or team. Include colleagues who were expected but couldn’t attend, so you know who needs an update afterward.

An attendance list helps readers identify who took part in a decision and who to contact for more context. For meetings with external participants, include their organization so colleagues understand their involvement.

Check the list when the meeting starts and update it if anyone joins later. If someone misses a discussion that affects their work, flag the relevant outcome when sharing the notes.

### 3. Capture agreements

During the discussion, focus on the main points, what was decided, and why. You don’t need to write down everything people say. Short phrases and bullet points help capture the information without losing track of the conversation.

Choose a note-taking method that suits the meeting:

- **Cornell method:** Divide your page into three sections: two columns with space for a summary underneath. During the meeting, write only in the right-hand column. Afterward, read through your notes and complete the other two sections:
  - **Notes column on the right:** Use the wider column to capture discussion points, decisions, and actions.
  - **Cue column on the left:** Beside each chunk of notes, add a keyword or short question that identifies what it covers. For notes about postponing a launch, for example, write “Launch date” or “Why was the launch postponed?”
  - **Summary area at the bottom:** Summarize the meeting in two or three sentences, drawing out the main decisions and next steps from your notes.

- **Outline method:** Use agenda items as headings, with bullet points underneath for related discussion, decisions, and actions. This works well when the meeting follows a clear sequence of topics.
- **Quadrant method:** Divide your page into four sections: Notes, Action Items, Questions, and Key Decisions. Keeping these separate helps you find the information you need when preparing the shared document.

Whichever method you use, be clear about what the team decided. If someone suggests postponing a launch but nobody agrees to it, don’t write that the launch has been postponed. Note that a postponement was discussed but no change was agreed, and record any agreed follow-up, such as checking when testing will be complete.

Before the meeting ends, read out the decisions you’ve recorded and ask participants to confirm they’re correct.

### 4. Define action items

Confirm each action before the meeting ends. Give the owner a chance to agree to the task and flag anything that would prevent them from completing it.

Each action should include:

- **Who:** Name one person responsible for moving the task forward. Other colleagues can contribute, but everyone should know who to contact for an update.
- **What:** Describe the work and what counts as finished. “Draft three launch-message options and share them with the team” gives the owner a clearer task than “Look into launch messaging.”
- **When:** Use an exact date rather than “later this week” or “ASAP,” so the deadline remains clear whenever someone reads the notes.

Record any dependencies, too. Someone preparing a cost comparison needs to know when the revised supplier quote will be available. Include who will provide it and by when, so the task owner knows what they’re waiting for.

## How to organize, share, and follow up on meeting notes

Once the meeting ends, your notes need to become a clear record that people can act on.

### Review and organize your notes

Review your notes soon after the meeting, while you still remember the discussion. Work through the following checks:

- **Clarify your notes:** Expand shorthand and confirm unclear decisions or responsibilities with the people involved.
- **Group related points:** Organize the document under topic headings. If the team returned to the budget several times, bring those points together in one place.
- **Remove unnecessary detail:** Cut repeated arguments, tangents, and comments that don’t explain the outcome. Keep the reasons behind decisions and any unresolved questions.
- **Make the document easy to scan:** Use short paragraphs and bullet points, with action items in a separate list or table.
- **Check for missing context:** Read the document from the perspective of someone who wasn’t there. Explain unfamiliar references and add any information needed to understand the discussion.

### Share your notes

Share the finished notes within 24 hours. Store the document in a shared, searchable workspace with a consistent naming format, such as “2026-10-12 — Email vendor renewal.”

Send a direct link rather than separate attachments so everyone refers to the same version. Before sending:

- **Check access:** Make sure the intended readers can open the document and restrict confidential information to the people who need it.
- **Include relevant absentees:** Add colleagues who missed the meeting but need the information or have work assigned to them.
- **Highlight the outcome:** Include the main decision and upcoming deadlines in your message.
- **Tag action owners:** Direct each person to their assigned tasks.
- **Request acknowledgment where needed:** For critical actions, ask the owner to confirm they’ve seen the task and deadline.

If a reader identifies an error in the notes, correct the shared document and notify anyone whose work is affected.

### Follow up on actions and decisions

Before the next related meeting, ask owners to update their tasks. Record what’s complete, what’s blocked, and any revised deadlines.

For stalled work, establish what’s preventing progress and add unresolved blockers to the next agenda. A missing approval, for example, needs to be addressed before the task can continue.

If the team changes an earlier decision, record the new agreement and link to the notes it replaces. Add a link in the original document pointing to the updated decision, so readers can identify which agreement is current.

## An example of effective meeting notes

The example below brings the four A’s together in a finished document, including the work required before the team can make a final decision.

**Meeting:** Email vendor renewal\
**Date:** October 12, 2026\
**Attendees:** Maya, Operations; Alex, IT; Priya, Finance\
**Absent:** Sam, Procurement

**Agenda:** Decide whether to renew the current email vendor before the October 30 renewal deadline.

**Discussion:** The team reviewed the proposed renewal price, current usage, and the work involved in switching providers. Finance needs a confirmed annual cost, while IT needs to check the cancellation notice period, data-export process, and the alternative provider’s security protections.

**Agreements:** Request a revised quote and compare it with one alternative provider before making the renewal decision.

**Action items:**

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Owner** | **Action** | **Deadline** |
| Maya | Request a revised annual quote from the current vendor. | October 14, 2026 |
| Alex | Confirm the cancellation notice period and document the data-export steps. | October 15, 2026 |
| Alex | Check whether the alternative provider meets the team’s security requirements and share the assessment. | October 16, 2026 |
| Priya | Compare the revised quote with the alternative and share a cost summary. | October 19, 2026 |

**Follow-up meeting:** October 21, 2026 — review the cost comparison and security assessment, then decide whether to renew or switch.

## Should you use AI for meeting notes?

AI meeting notes tools record and transcribe discussions, while a [business AI assistant](https://proton.me/business/lumo) can prepare summaries from written notes and pull out action items.

Ask participants for permission before using a tool to record or transcribe the meeting, and check how the service handles your data before recording or uploading notes. A recording could include client information, internal plans, or employee concerns, even if you leave those details out of the final summary. For each service you’re considering, find out:

- **What you need to share:** Whether the tool needs the full recording or transcript, and whether you can leave out sensitive details.
- **How the information is used:** Whether recordings, transcripts, or summaries are used to train models or for purposes beyond preparing your notes.
- **Who can access it:** Your team, the provider, and other companies involved in processing or reviewing the content. A promise not to train AI on your data doesn’t establish who can read it.
- **What’s stored:** How long the service retains recordings, transcripts, summaries, and processing logs, and what you can delete.
- **How it’s protected:** What encryption covers and whether the provider holds the keys needed to read stored information.

If your meeting notes contain confidential information, [Lumo for Business](https://proton.me/business/lumo) offers AI assistance without using your conversations to train models. Saved chat history is protected with zero-access encryption, so even Proton can’t read it.

AI can and does get things wrong. Once you’ve generated a summary, check it against your original notes or recording before sharing. Pay particular attention to:

- **Decisions:** Confirm that suggestions or unresolved discussions haven’t been presented as agreements.
- **Responsibilities:** Check that each person named as an owner accepted the task.
- **Details:** Verify names, figures, and deadlines against the original discussion.
- **Missing context:** Make sure the summary explains the reasons behind important decisions.

## Your meeting notes cheat sheet

Taking effective meeting notes means capturing decisions, assigning clear actions, and following up on progress. Ready to put this into practice? We’ve got you covered with a quick checklist to keep handy before, during, and after your next meeting:

- **Before:** Agree who will take notes and prepare a document with the title, date, and agenda headings.
- **During:** Record attendance, decisions and their reasons, and action items with named owners and deadlines.
- **Before everyone leaves:** Confirm the decisions and ask action owners to acknowledge their tasks.
- **Within 24 hours:** Check and organize your notes, then share a link with the people who need them.
- **Before the next meeting:** Review progress, identify blockers, and add outstanding issues to the agenda.
