Travel creates a trail of personal data. A smartphone can reveal your [location](https://www.ap.org/news-highlights/best-of-the-week/2022/tech-tool-police-track-movement/) through mobile networks, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, apps, and background activity. Tickets and card payments can connect your identity to a journey, while cameras may record where you go. Even finding accommodation can mean handing over personal details.

Reducing that trail takes planning. You can leave your phone switched off, use cash, opt for paper tickets, carry offline entertainment, and choose privacy-focused tools when you need to use a computer. But each workaround comes with its own limits and public surveillance is difficult to avoid entirely.

The aim is to give away as little information as possible, while understanding what each choice costs in time, money, and convenience.

## Leave your smartphone behind

A smartphone is one of the most persistent sources of location data that you carry and GPS is only part of the picture. Your device can communicate with mobile towers, Wi-Fi networks, and Bluetooth devices, while apps and online services may collect location and activity information in the background.

Over time, separate traces can reveal patterns: where you live and work, the places you visit, who you meet, and when you tend to be there. The concern is less about one isolated signal than the profile that can emerge when many records are linked.

For a trip where minimizing data collection is the priority, the simplest option is to switch the phone off and keep it isolated in a [Faraday bag](https://proton.me/blog/privacy-gadgets). This is a pouch designed to block wireless signals. A makeshift version using layers of metal foil can be unreliable if there are gaps, so it should not be treated as a guaranteed shield.

That said, leaving your phone behind has an immediate downside. You lose easy access to maps, messages, bookings, mobile payments, and live travel updates. A paper map and printed timetables can cover some of those needs, but you have to plan ahead and ask people for help when plans change.

## Use cash and paper tickets where possible

A card payment can create a timestamped record linking a person to a merchant, location, amount, and time. That information may be held across several organizations, including the merchant, bank, card network, and payment processor. Paying with a phone or smartwatch can add another account or device to the transaction.

A series of purchases can reveal more than any one transaction. It may show where you stopped, how your route changed, and what kinds of places you visited. Cash reduces the digital record created at the point of purchase, and it can also help keep individual purchases out of loyalty-program profiles.

There are trade-offs though. Some businesses no longer accept cash, carrying it can be inconvenient, and you may need to withdraw more along the way. An ATM withdrawal is still recorded by your bank and may be captured on CCTV, so cash is not completely anonymous. It simply separates that withdrawal record from the details of each later purchase.

The same principle applies to transport. Buying a paper ticket at a counter and paying cash, when accepted, avoids some of the account, app, and payment data associated with booking online. But the journey itself may still be recorded by station and onboard cameras. Paper tickets reduce one part of the trail, not all of it.

## Plan for the data trail around public transport

A bus can seem like a low-tech way to travel, but the surrounding systems may generate plenty of data. Journey-planning apps, live tracking, online tickets, and contactless payments can reveal where you are, where you intend to go, and when your plans change.

Printed timetables and maps are useful alternatives. Ask staff or local residents about routes and connections, and allow extra time for delays. This is less convenient than checking an app, but it avoids generating some location searches and account activity.

Trains create a more formal record of travel. A ticket can connect a route, destination, time, and payment method, while CCTV may capture you at the station and onboard. If you need to take a train, buying a paper ticket in person and paying cash where possible can reduce the amount of data tied directly to your identity. It will not, however, make your trip invisible.

Free [public Wi-Fi](https://protonvpn.com/blog/public-wifi-safety) can be tempting when you need to check a connection or find accommodation. Connecting may associate your device with a particular network and location, and a sign-in page can ask for identifying details. Poorly secured networks can also expose users to security risks. When possible, stay offline and use printed information or ask someone for help. If you do need to connect, avoid assuming that a network is trustworthy simply because it is free.

## Find accommodation without oversharing

Accommodation can be one of the hardest parts of a low-data journey. Booking platforms may collect names, email addresses, phone numbers, payment details, IP addresses, and account activity. At the property, there may also be ID checks, CCTV, vehicle records, and guest Wi-Fi logins.

These records can connect your identity with your travel dates and where you are staying. They can also create risks if booking or hotel systems are compromised, since scammers may be able to use travel details to make fraudulent messages more convincing.

A lower-data approach is to consult a printed guide, contact properties directly, and ask what information they require before you arrive. Paying cash may be possible at some places, but it is increasingly common to encounter card-only policies or online booking requirements. Calling ahead can save time, although a public phone or someone else’s phone may be necessary if you have left your own device offline.

If a property asks for identification, read its privacy policy where available and ask how the information will be used, who can access it, and how long it will be retained. You do not need to assume every request is sinister. The point is to understand what you are handing over and avoid providing extra information without a clear reason.

## Cameras can (and will) record your journey

Avoiding digital services does not mean avoiding surveillance. CCTV is common in stations, shops, car parks, and other public or privately managed spaces. A single camera sighting may reveal little by itself, but records can become more revealing when footage is linked across locations or combined with other information.

Some retailers also use live [facial recognition](https://www.reuters.com/world/uk/londons-streets-facial-recognition-tests-balance-between-security-liberty-2026-05-22/) to compare people entering a store against a watchlist. This raises a different concern from ordinary CCTV because the system attempts to identify a person in real time. Errors can have consequences, particularly if someone is challenged based on a mistaken match.

You cannot reliably opt out of cameras in public places. You can, however, make informed choices about where you shop and which services you use, while recognizing that avoiding one camera may mean taking a longer route or spending more time finding an alternative. Do not treat disguises or improvised signal-blocking methods as guarantees of anonymity.

## Driving creates a different kind of data trail

A car may feel more private than public transport, but it brings its own records. Automated [number plate recognition systems](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2018/11/eff-and-muckrock-release-records-and-data-200-law-enforcement-agencies-automated) can capture vehicles on roads and in car parks. Hiring a car also links the journey to rental paperwork, identification, and payment information.

Connected vehicles may collect details about journeys and driving behavior, depending on the vehicle and its settings. Such information can sometimes be shared beyond the manufacturer or driver, including with third parties involved in insurance or data services. A vehicle can therefore reveal more than its location.

Taking a lift from someone you trust avoids the booking trail of a rental car, but it does not remove vehicle surveillance. Changing routes may reduce exposure to some cameras, though it cannot guarantee that a car will not be recorded somewhere else. The idea is to recognize the data associated with each option rather than assume one mode of transport is private by default.

## Use a public computer carefully with Tails and Tor

Sometimes you need to get online to research a route, find a place to stay, or check essential information. A public computer can provide access without reconnecting your own smartphone, but it is still a shared device. You should avoid entering sensitive account details and should not assume the computer is safe simply because it is in a library or public space.

Tails is an operating system designed to run from a USB drive and leave as little trace as possible on the computer after use. It routes internet connections through Tor by default. This can be useful when you need a temporary computing environment, but it does not make every action anonymous. Signing into a personal account can identify you to that service, and the computer itself may not be trustworthy.

Tor sends internet traffic through multiple relays, making it harder for a website to see your real IP address. The [Tor Browser](https://protonvpn.com/blog/what-is-tor-browser) also includes protections against some forms of tracking and browser fingerprinting. It is not a magic invisibility cloak: behavior that identifies you, logging into personal accounts, or unsafe downloads can undermine your privacy. Use the official Tor Browser and follow the Tor Project’s [best practices](https://support.torproject.org/tor-browser/security/using-tb-safely/).

## Privacy means choosing your trade-offs

Traveling with a smaller data trail is possible, but complete anonymity is difficult in a world where payments, transport, accommodation, and public spaces are increasingly connected. Leaving a smartphone behind can cut out a major source of tracking, yet it also makes everyday tasks slower and less predictable. Cash helps at the checkout, but it cannot prevent CCTV or remove the record of an ATM withdrawal.

Decide which information you most want to protect, plan around the services you need, and make deliberate choices about when convenience is worth the data it creates. The goal is to reduce unnecessary exposure, not to assume that every trace can be erased.
