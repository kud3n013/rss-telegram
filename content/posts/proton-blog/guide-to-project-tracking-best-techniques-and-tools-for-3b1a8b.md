Project tracking can quickly get out of hand without the right tools and strategies. Suddenly budgets don’t tally, key milestones are missed, and people don’t know what they’re responsible for completing. You need a project tracker.

We share why project tracking is important, how to tell if a project is meeting key metrics, and best practices to meet your deadline. You’ll also learn how to manage multiple projects simultaneously, even if you don’t have access to a dedicated project management software (hint: an [online spreadsheet editor](https://proton.me/business/drive/sheets) enables you to do exactly that).

## What is project tracking?

Project tracking is the process of monitoring your project’s progress against your original plan. It involves tracking tasks, deadlines, budget, and resources to keep everything moving in the right direction. You can use project tracking at any scale, from managing family and home life to large commercial projects.

## Why is tracking project progress important?

Trying to execute a project without tracking your progress is like building something without a measuring tape. You can eyeball it and hope for the best, but it’s pretty unlikely to work out well. Here’s how your project can benefit from tracking:

### Catching problems before they escalate

Real-time visibility is essential, especially if your project has dependencies. Unblock team members, enable collaboration, and facilitate a bigger-picture approach. If you know your key milestones and when they need to be achieved, it’s easy to see when things fall off track. The earlier you catch a problem, the easier it is to address.

### Making progress faster

“This meeting could have been an email” could just as easily be rephrased to “This meeting could have been a spreadsheet.” You can avoid lengthy status meetings if a glance at the project tracker shows who’s doing what and whether it’s on track.

### Staying on budget

Your tracker should make it easy to see if your budget is anticipated to blow out. Tracking projects allows you to react quickly to keep the budget in check.

## Key metrics every project tracker should include

Using established project management methodologies will give you the data you need to achieve your goals as efficiently as possible. To get the most out of your project tracker, you should include the following project management metrics:

### Timeline and milestone tracker

Milestones represent major phase completions, such as prototype approval or design sign-off, rather than individual tasks. Plot your fixed dates and create a timeline anchored around key milestones. This enables you to easily see when things are off track, and it’s an opportunity to employ some project management jargon. **Schedule variance (SV)** is the difference between planned and actual progress at any given point.

Tracking milestones lets you calculate SV visually without complex formulas. For a [spreadsheet setup](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1-GE_c8BNKgsBzOQcMpY78g8EZ_D-kYmoPF7yn9Wjgjg/edit?tab=t.0#heading=h.cgcanpx0d5b), you can use conditional formatting to highlight incomplete milestones with a completion date that’s already passed. This flags slippage before it becomes a crisis.

### Budget

Create a detailed budget that allows you to itemize and track expenses in real-time. Your tracker will need columns for estimated cost per task, actual cost per task, and a running total. For smaller projects, a simple comparison of estimated vs actual totals might be sufficient. For larger undertakings, you could consider adding some additional formatting.

If you’re going to take this to the next level, you can create a **Cost Performance Index (CPI)**. This combines cost variance (whether you’re over or under budget) and burn rate (how quickly your funds are being consumed relative to the timeline) to give you a CPI value. Anything below 1.0 means you’re burning cash faster than you’re completing work, and it’s time to reassess.

### Issues and risks log

Create a spreadsheet to identify known issues or risks. You’ll need two data points for each risk: first, rate the probability of each risk on a scale of 1-5, then rate impact severity on the same scale. You can then rank your issues by creating a **risk scoring framework**. This is calculated by multiplying probability x impact.

Risk scoring is standard practice in PMBOK and PRINCE2 and gives you a priority ranking that guides which issues need active monitoring and which you can observe passively. Once you’ve ranked your risks, you can also outline your mitigation strategies in the same document.

### Task completion status

Create statuses that reflect your workflow (common categories include Not Started, In Progress, In Review, Done) and link tasks to phases or milestones. Once each task has a phase and a status, you can see the **percent completed by phase** by calculating the percentage of tasks marked Done in that phase. If you want to reflect the work that’s In Progress as well, you can assign it a partial value.

Stakeholder reporting becomes a lot easier when tasks are tied to milestones, and you can see which phases are dragging and whether milestone targets are realistic.

## How to manage multiple projects at once

The good news is that managing several projects at once doesn’t mean multiplying your effort — you can simply duplicate and modify as needed. Give each project its own tab in your spreadsheet, built from the same template. If every project uses identical columns, statuses, and formulas, you never have to relearn where anything lives.

Add a roll-up dashboard on a separate tab that pulls headline figures from each project: percent complete, overdue task count, and budget status. These are simple cross-sheet formulas, and one row per project is enough to see at a glance where your attention is most needed.

**Tip:** Resist the urge to consolidate tasks from different projects into one giant task list. Context switching is fatiguing and inefficient, and separate tabs will keep individual projects legible.

## 5 tips for project tracking from a program manager at Proton

We asked a program manager at Proton how they keep complex, cross-team projects on track using nothing more than a well-built spreadsheet. Here’s what they told us.

### 1. Set up a single project tracking log

Start by breaking the project into milestones, then break each milestone into tasks. Use the header row to define exactly what you need to track for each one: milestone or task, owner, start date, due date, live date, status, and links to relevant documents.

The point is to keep everything in one sheet. When the tracker is the single source of truth, nobody has to dig through email or chat threads to find the latest version, and every question about the project has one place to go for an answer.

### 2. Track your completion rate over time

Spreadsheet formulas only show the current value. Your percent complete today overwrites what it was last week, so you lose the history that tells you whether the project is speeding up or stalling.

Fix this with a simple log table. Add two minutes to your weekly standup to record that week’s percent complete. After a few weeks, the log becomes a trend line that shows progress at a glance. It also doubles as your status report, so you’re not writing one from scratch every Friday.

### 3. Build a Gantt-style timeline with conditional formatting

You don’t need dedicated project management software to get a timeline view. Lay out columns as weeks or days, then add conditional formatting rules that shade each cell based on the task’s start and end dates.

Because the shading is driven by the dates, the timeline updates itself whenever a date changes. There’s nothing to redraw. Add a second rule that highlights overdue tasks in a different color, and slipping deadlines will stand out the moment they happen, not at the next review.

### 4. Store the sheet in a shared Drive folder

A tracker only works if everyone is looking at the same copy. Keep it in a Proton Drive folder shared with the project team, so every collaborator works from one end-to-end encrypted file instead of downloaded duplicates.

Set permissions to match each person’s role: owners as editors, stakeholders as viewers. You can apply these at the file or folder level. If something changes unexpectedly, version history shows you who changed what and when, and lets you roll back if needed.

### 5. Use data validation to keep your data clean

Free-text fields are where trackers fall apart. One person types “In progress,” another types “in prog,” a third types “ongoing”. Suddenly your filters miss half the tasks and your status charts are wrong.

Add dropdown menus to your status, priority, and owner columns using data validation. When people pick from a fixed list, your data stays consistent, so sorting, filtering, and any status-based formulas or charts stay accurate as the project grows.

### Start simple, then build

You don’t need all five at once. Start with a single tracking log and dropdowns for status, since those two alone will eliminate most of the confusion. Add the completion log and timeline view once the basics are in place.

## FAQ on project tracking

##### What’s the difference between project tracking and project management?

Project management covers the full discipline: scoping, planning, resourcing, and executing a project. Project tracking is one ongoing component of that process — monitoring progress against your plan so tasks, deadlines, budget, and risks stay visible. You can track a project without dedicated project management software; the discipline matters more than the tool.

##### How do I manage multiple projects without dedicated project management tools?

Structure is what makes multi-project tracking possible, not software. Build each project as its own tab using the same template — identical columns, statuses, and formulas — then create a dashboard tab with one row per project pulling percent complete, overdue tasks, and budget status. Consistent structure means your effort scales across projects without multiplying.

##### What free tools can I use for project management tracking?

A [spreadsheet app](https://proton.me/business/drive/sheets) paired with [business cloud storage](https://proton.me/business/drive) covers most project tracking needs. Formulas, conditional formatting, data validation, and charts for tracking; encrypted storage for your files and folders.

Proton Sheets and Proton Drive offer both with end-to-end encryption, and Proton’s [business AI assistant](https://proton.me/business/lumo), Lumo, can help you build your tracking template from scratch.

##### What should a project tracker include?

At minimum, a project tracker should include a timeline with key milestones, an itemized budget, an issues and risks log, and task completion statuses linked to project phases. Together, these give you the core metrics (schedule variance, budget position, and percent complete by phase) that show whether a project is on track.

##### Can I use a spreadsheet for project tracking?

Yes, a spreadsheet supports everything essential to project tracking, including tasks lists with statuses and owners, deadlines with automatic overdue flagging, budget columns with running totals, and charts for visualizing progress. Spreadsheets work especially well for small to medium projects, and a well-built one can be saved as a template for reuse across your team.
